A few years ago, "AI" in a job description usually meant a specialist team training models. In 2026 it's closer to electricity: something almost every team plugs into, whether they build models or not.

I wanted to understand what that actually looks like inside tech companies, beyond the headlines, so this post pulls together some of the clearest numbers I could find and what they mean for someone about to start their career.

![Bar chart of AI adoption figures from Stack Overflow, McKinsey and Google](images/ai-adoption-chart.svg)

## Adoption is nearly universal

McKinsey's [State of AI 2025](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/tech-and-ai/our-insights/the-state-of-ai-2025) survey found that 88% of organisations now regularly use AI in at least one business function, up from 78% the year before. Among developers specifically, the [2025 Stack Overflow Developer Survey](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/ai) found that 84% use or plan to use AI tools in their work, and about half of professional developers use them every day.

But using AI and getting value from it are different things. McKinsey also reported that only about a third of organisations have started scaling AI across the business, and most of those seeing a financial impact said it accounted for less than 5% of earnings.

## Where AI is actually being used

### 1. Writing and reviewing code

This is the most visible change. In April 2026 Google said that around [75% of its new code is now generated by AI](https://www.techspot.com/news/112152-google-ai-now-generates-75-new-code-up.html) and then reviewed by engineers, up from roughly a quarter in late 2024. The important word is *reviewed*. Engineers aren't disappearing; their work is shifting towards specifying problems clearly, reviewing output and owning the result.

Typical uses include:

- generating boilerplate, tests and documentation,
- explaining unfamiliar code during onboarding,
- large migrations and refactors that would be tedious by hand,
- first-pass code review that flags likely bugs.

### 2. AI agents

The newest step is **agents**: AI that doesn't just suggest a line of code but plans and carries out a multi-step task, such as running tests, reading the errors and trying a fix. McKinsey found that 39% of organisations have begun experimenting with agents, and Stack Overflow found that about 31% of developers already use them at work in some form.

### 3. Customer support

Support was one of the earliest business areas to adopt AI at scale. Assistants now answer routine questions, draft replies for human agents and summarise long ticket histories, so people can focus on the cases that need judgement.

### 4. Search, recommendations and personalisation

The feeds, search results and "you might also like" lists in most apps are driven by machine learning. This is older than the generative AI boom, but large language models have made it possible to search by *meaning* rather than exact keywords.

### 5. Security and operations

AI helps teams spot unusual activity in logs, triage alerts and detect fraud in real time. When a system produces millions of events a day, no human team can read them all.

## The trust gap

Here's the part that I think gets too little attention. In the same Stack Overflow survey, more developers said they *distrust* the accuracy of AI output (about 46%) than trust it (about 33%), and the most experienced developers were the most cautious. Overall favourable sentiment actually dropped compared with previous years.

That isn't a contradiction. It reflects how AI is really being used: as a fast, capable assistant whose work still has to be checked. AI can produce code that looks right, compiles, and is still subtly wrong, especially around security, edge cases and business rules.

> AI makes writing code cheaper. It doesn't make *being wrong* any cheaper.

## What this means for junior developers

There's a real worry that AI will remove entry-level roles. I don't think the honest answer is "nothing will change". But from what I've seen, the skills that make a junior developer valuable are shifting rather than vanishing:

- **Fundamentals.** You can't review AI output if you don't understand what correct looks like. Data structures, HTTP, databases and security basics matter more, not less.
- **Testing.** When code is generated quickly, tests are what tell you whether it works. Writing xUnit and Jest tests on my projects has made me much more comfortable using AI tools safely.
- **Specifying problems clearly.** Good prompts look a lot like good requirements.
- **Security awareness.** Knowing why you hash passwords, validate input and enforce roles on the server lets you catch the mistakes an assistant might make.
- **Communication.** Translating between what a client needs and what the system does is still a deeply human job.

## My approach

I use AI tools the way I'd use a very fast pair programmer: great for getting started, explaining unfamiliar code and generating tests, but never the final word. Every line still has to pass review, tests and my own understanding before it goes in.

The companies getting the most out of AI seem to work the same way: they pair it with strong engineering practices instead of treating it as a replacement for them.
